SECTION 1 — LIGHTING MANAGEMENT IN EDUCATIONAL BUILDINGS

1.1 Occupancy-Based Lighting Control
Switching off artificial lighting in unoccupied rooms is one of the most
cost-effective energy conservation measures available to educational
institutions. Spaces such as classrooms, seminar halls, and offices that
operate on predictable schedules are ideal candidates for occupancy-sensor
control or timed switching. Studies of university buildings in India suggest
that lighting in general-purpose rooms is left on for an average of 2–4 hours
per day beyond actual occupancy, representing 15–25% of lighting energy
consumption.

Recommended practice: Install occupancy sensors with a 15-minute timeout in
classrooms and offices. Manual override should always be available to occupants.
Do not apply automatic shut-off to laboratory fume cupboard lighting or
emergency egress luminaires.

1.2 Lighting Power Density Targets
The Bureau of Energy Efficiency (BEE) Star Labelling program sets reference
Lighting Power Density (LPD) values for educational buildings:
  Classroom:    10 W/m²
  Office:        9 W/m²
  Laboratory:   15 W/m²
  Corridor:      5 W/m²

Actual LPD above these reference values indicates an opportunity to upgrade
to more efficient luminaires (e.g., LED) without reducing illuminance levels.

SECTION 2 — HVAC AND THERMAL COMFORT

2.1 Temperature Setpoints
Maintaining HVAC setpoints within the comfort range of 24–26 °C (cooling)
and 20–22 °C (heating) has been shown to reduce energy consumption by up to
18% compared with under-controlled systems. Each degree of unnecessary cooling
increases compressor energy use by approximately 3–5%.

2.2 Unoccupied Setback
When a room is unoccupied for more than 30 minutes, HVAC systems should enter
setback mode (e.g., 28 °C cooling setpoint) rather than maintaining full
comfort conditions. This single measure can account for 10–20% of annual HVAC
energy savings in Indian university buildings where HVAC run-hours often
extend 2–4 hours beyond occupancy.

2.3 Ventilation and CO2 Monitoring
CO2 concentration above 1000 ppm in an occupied space indicates inadequate
ventilation. Demand-controlled ventilation (DCV) adjusts outdoor air flow
based on measured CO2, avoiding over-ventilation of partially occupied rooms.
Maintaining CO2 below 800 ppm during full occupancy and allowing a rise to
1000–1200 ppm threshold before increasing ventilation can reduce fan energy
by 20–30% in variable-occupancy spaces.

SECTION 3 — ENERGY METERING AND MONITORING

3.1 Sub-Metering
Installing electrical sub-meters at the room or zone level enables waste
identification that aggregate building meters cannot provide. Key metering
points for educational buildings:
  - Each HVAC unit (chiller, AHU, split)
  - Lighting circuits per floor or zone
  - Laboratory plug loads
  - Server rooms and UPS systems

3.2 Anomaly Detection
Baseline energy profiles for each room type allow automated detection of
anomalous consumption. Deviations greater than 20% above the 7-day rolling
average during the same occupancy period should trigger an investigation.

SECTION 4 — LABORATORY AND CRITICAL EQUIPMENT

4.1 Safety Considerations
Laboratory equipment including fume hoods, biological safety cabinets,
environmental chambers, and server racks must NOT be de-energised as part of
general energy-saving measures. These systems may be required for safety,
specimen preservation, or continuous experiments. Any power management in
laboratories must be coordinated with the laboratory manager.

4.2 Plug Load Management
Non-critical plug loads (monitors, phone chargers, coffee machines) in
laboratories and offices represent 15–30% of plug-load energy. Smart power
strips with occupancy-linked switching can reduce this without affecting
critical equipment.

----------------------------------------------------------------------

SECTION 5 — DATA QUALITY AND REPORTING

5.1 Measurement vs. Estimation
Energy efficiency reporting should clearly distinguish between:
  (a) Measured savings — derived from sub-meter before/after comparisons.
  (b) Estimated savings — calculated from engineering assumptions and
      published typical values.
  (c) Simulated savings — generated by a model without measurement.

Estimated and simulated savings should always state their assumptions,
uncertainty range, and the basis for the calculation.

5.2 Missing Data
When occupancy or energy data is unavailable, do not extrapolate savings
claims. Instead, report the data gap and recommend installing the necessary
instrumentation.
